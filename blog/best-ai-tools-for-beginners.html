How to evaluate AI tools: a beginner's practical checklist

There is no single “best” AI tool for every person. A writing assistant, video editor and automation platform solve different problems. Product rankings also become outdated as features, free-plan limits and prices change.

Instead of choosing from a long list, use the same small test for each candidate.

1. Define the task and success criteria

Write down one task you want to complete, the input you can safely provide and what an acceptable output looks like. Example: “Turn a 500-word public product brief into five FAQ questions, with no claims beyond the supplied brief.” This is easier to assess than “make my work faster.”

2. Compare categories, not marketing slogans

CategoryUseful test taskWhat to inspect
Text assistantOutline or summarize supplied textAccuracy, citations, tone, revision control
Design editorAdapt a layout to two sizesTypography, export quality, asset rights
Video editorTranscribe and caption a short clipWord accuracy, timing, audio and watermark
Automation platformRoute a sample form to a draft taskError handling, permissions, logs and rollback

3. Run a fair side-by-side test

  1. Choose two or three candidate tools in the same category.
  2. Use the same sample input and instruction.
  3. Score each output against your criteria, not its visual polish.
  4. Repeat once with a different sample to check consistency.
  5. Record how much human correction was needed.

For a text tool, ask it to separate facts drawn from the supplied material from assumptions, and to mark anything requiring external verification. Never treat a generated reference or statistic as verified without checking the original source.

Case study: Comparing two AI writing assistants on the same product brief

Test date: 11 November 2026. This is a small, single-task comparison, not a general ranking of AI products. The ChatGPT response was generated in a GPT-6 session. The Gemini response was supplied from a user-run test; its exact model version and account tier were not recorded.

Test brief

Both assistants received the same fictional product information for a reusable stainless-steel desk bottle: 500 ml capacity, screw-on lid, black/white/blue color options, hand-wash recommendation, and a package containing one bottle and one lid. Price, warranty and insulation were not specified.

The shared instruction was to write exactly five customer-facing FAQs, keep each answer between 25 and 45 words, use only the supplied facts, avoid unsupported claims, identify missing information, and list details the store owner should confirm before publication.

What the responses did well

  • Both produced five FAQs and a separate list of details to confirm.
  • Both avoided claiming that the bottle was insulated, leakproof, certified or covered by a warranty.
  • ChatGPT explicitly described the screw-on lid and flagged that leak resistance was unknown.
  • Gemini covered the material and warranty directly, and suggested confirming dimensions and weight.

Issues found during review

  • The ChatGPT response mentioned that color availability could depend on stock. This is a general possibility, not a fact in the supplied brief.
  • The Gemini response described the capacity as “up to 500 ml”; the brief stated a capacity of 500 ml, so the wording adds a qualification that was not supplied.
  • The Gemini response did not explicitly mention that the lid is screw-on, although this detail was in the brief.
  • Both responses used some light promotional or contextual wording that a store owner should review before publication.

What this test suggests

ChatGPT scored slightly higher in this particular exercise because it surfaced the screw-on lid and the unknown leak-resistance detail. Gemini offered useful coverage of material, warranty and dimensions. The difference was small, and one task is not enough to conclude that either assistant is generally better.

The practical lesson is to check every generated product statement against the source brief. Fluent wording does not make an assumption into a verified product fact. A human editor should confirm missing specifications before publishing customer-facing copy.

Limitations: This was one fictional product brief and one response from each assistant. The Gemini model version was not captured, and the scoring involved editorial judgment. More tasks and repeated runs would be needed for a broader comparison.

4. Check the free plan carefully

  • Usage quotas, rate limits and reset periods.
  • Watermarks, resolution limits and export restrictions.
  • Whether commercial use is allowed and what attribution is required.
  • How uploaded prompts and files are stored or used.
  • Whether team features, integrations or essential formats require payment.
  • The price after a trial or when usage exceeds the free allowance.

5. Protect data and rights

Use public or synthetic sample data during evaluation. Do not upload customer records, private correspondence, credentials or confidential business material unless you have authorization and have reviewed the provider's data terms. Confirm rights for generated images, music, fonts, templates and other assets before commercial use.

6. Calculate total effort

A tool that generates a draft in seconds may still require substantial checking. Compare subscription costs plus setup, editing, fact-checking, exporting and maintenance time. Keep the tool only if it improves the complete workflow.

Reusable scorecard

Give each candidate a score from 1 (poor) to 5 (strong) for task quality, consistency, ease of review, privacy fit, export options and total cost. Add a note explaining each score. Do not combine scores until you decide which criteria matter most for your task.

Features and terms change. Check the provider's official documentation and pricing before making a purchase.
